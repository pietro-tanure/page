# The Long Nineteenth Century — Condensed Bullet Notes
### Undergraduate Course Notes — With Citations

*Note: prioritizes analytical depth over strict timing — trim per section for a 40-min oral delivery, or assign as reading.*

---

## 0. Theoretical Framework
- Historical materialism: base (mode of production, class relations) shapes superstructure (politics, culture)
- History driven by contradiction: forces of production vs. relations of production (Marx & Engels 1848; Marx 1867)
- Periodization: Hobsbawm's "long 19th century" (1789–1914), not a hard 1800–1900 cut
  - Age of Revolution: 1789–1848
  - Age of Capital: 1848–1875
  - Age of Empire: 1875–1914
  - (Hobsbawm 1962; 1975; 1987)
- "Dual revolution" thesis: French political revolution + British industrial revolution = engine of the century (Hobsbawm 1962)
- Not an exclusive lens:
  - Liberal/nationalist: stresses ideas, individual agency (Bismarck, Cavour)
  - World-systems/dependency (Wallerstein 1974–89; Rodney 1972): core–periphery exchange over class struggle

---

## Overview: 11 Major Transformations of the 19th Century

1. **Creation of nation-states** — Italy, Germany; consolidation of others
2. **Industrial Revolution** — First (steam/textiles/rail); Second (steel/electricity/chemicals, from the 1870s)
3. **Abolition of slavery and serfdom** — staggered/uneven: British Empire (1833), French colonies (1794, reversed 1802, re-abolished 1848), US (1865), Russian serfdom (1861), Brazil (1888, last in the Americas)
4. **Imperialism / colonial expansion** — Scramble for Africa, forced opening of China and Japan, formal empire in India — globalization of capitalism into a core–periphery structure
5. **Formation of the modern proletariat and organized labor** — factory working class as a new social category, trade unions, First and Second Internationals, mass socialist parties
6. **Urbanization and demographic transformation** — mass rural-to-urban migration, population boom (public health/agricultural improvements), mass transoceanic emigration (Europe → Americas)
7. **Transportation/communication revolution** — railways, steamships, the telegraph — compresses time and space, integrates a genuine world market, enables both trade and colonial control
8. **Expansion and bureaucratization of the state** — mass conscript armies, census-taking and statistics, public education systems, professional civil administration
9. **Partial extension of political rights** — expanding (male) suffrage, written constitutions — partial/contested, since workers and women remain excluded almost everywhere until the century's end or later
10. **Intellectual/scientific rupture** — Darwinism, positivism, broader secularization of authority competing with religious worldviews
11. **Crystallization of competing political ideologies** — liberalism, conservatism, socialism, nationalism, anarchism — as rival programmatic responses to everything above

**Rough dialectical shape:**
- **Base (economic):** 3, 4, 6, 7 — labor systems, resource/market expansion, physical infrastructure of accumulation
- **Political-institutional form:** 1, 8 — the state forms that base takes
- **Superstructure/ideological response:** 5, 9, 10, 11 — new classes and ideas generated by, and acting back on, the economic transformation

## 1. Preconditions: Bourgeois Revolutions Before 1800

**English Revolution (1640s–1688)**
- Civil War (1642–51) → execution of Charles I (1649) → Commonwealth/Cromwell → Restoration (1660) → Glorious Revolution (1688)
- Two waves: 1649–60 reforms (republic, abolished Lords, religious radicalism) mostly *reversed* at Restoration; narrower changes below *survive* — these are what matter structurally

*Ending feudal land tenure*
- Tenures Abolition Act (1660): abolishes knight-service tenure, the Court of Wards (crown profits from managing minors' estates), and purveyance (crown's below-market compulsory purchase) → land becomes free/common socage — fully alienable private property
  - Benefits landed gentry/aristocracy; land becomes a liquid, capital-like commodity (Hill 1972; Anderson 1974)

*Ending arbitrary crown fiscal/judicial power*
- Ship Money crisis (1637–38, Hampden's Case) → levy abolished 1641: ends crown taxation without parliamentary consent
- Star Chamber abolished (1641): ends crown power to fine/imprison/seize property outside common law
- Triennial Act (1641): forces regular Parliaments, ends Charles I's 1629–40 "Personal Rule"
- Bill of Rights (1689): bars suspending laws/taxing without Parliament; bars a peacetime standing army without consent
  - Benefits the propertied parliamentary class: predictable property rights + taxation = precondition for long-horizon investment

*Enabling merchant capital*
- Statute of Monopolies (1624): voids crown-granted trade monopolies (except patents) → opens markets beyond court favorites
- Navigation Acts (1651, 1660, 1663): require English ships/crews for trade; colonial goods routed through England first
  - Benefits English merchants/shipowners at Dutch expense — mercantilism as state power aligned with commercial capital

*Financial infrastructure*
- Bank of England Act (1694): charters a private bank, funds government debt at interest, note-issuing privilege
  - Founds the permanent "fiscal-military state" (Brewer); City financiers gain; state can now outspend fiscally weaker rivals (Anderson 1974)

*Dispossession into wage labor*
- Parliamentary enclosure (accelerating late 17th–19th c., thousands of individual Enclosure Acts): converts open-field/common land into fenced private farms; Parliament, by now controlled by the same landowning class, legislates it
  - Smallholders/cottagers lose subsistence access to common land → pushed into wage labor = Marx's "primitive accumulation" as a legislated fact (*Capital* Vol. 1, ch. 27)

*Religious/ideological complement*
- Test Act (1673): excludes Catholics/non-Anglicans from public office
- Toleration Act (1689): grants worship freedom to Protestant dissenters, without full civic rights
  - Stabilizes the Protestant propertied coalition; Weber's Protestant work-ethic thesis as a complementary, non-material argument (Weber 1905)

*Agents*
- 1640s–50s: parliamentary gentry, London mercantile/financial elite (vs. crown monopolies), Puritan clergy/laity, New Model Army
  - Internal conflict: Levellers (Putney Debates, 1647 — manhood suffrage, legal equality) and Diggers (agrarian communism) suppressed by Cromwell/the Grandees → shows leadership relocated sovereignty crown→propertied Parliament, not abolished hierarchy
- 1688: narrower elite coalition — Whig/Tory aristocrats + City financiers invite William of Orange; Dutch commercial-capitalist institutions (funded public debt, merchant oligarchy) imported with him; no Levellers this time

*Interests at play*
- Gentry/landowners: property security, freedom from arbitrary taxation/feudal exaction
- Merchant capital: end crown monopolies, predictable commercial law, state-protected trade routes (though chartered monopolists like the East India Company wanted to keep their *own* monopoly)
- Financiers (by 1690s): reliable public credit via parliamentary tax oversight
- Puritan/dissenting interests: freedom from Anglican hierarchy; elective affinity with capitalist accumulation ethic (Weber 1905)
- Radical fringe (Levellers/Diggers): wanted to extend the revolution further (political equality, common land) — defeated *by* the propertied revolutionary coalition, not the royalists → key evidence for the class-character claim

*Adaptation chain to capitalism*
1. Tenures Abolition (1660) → land as a freely alienable commodity
2. End of prerogative courts + parliamentary tax control (1641, 1689) → legal predictability for investment
3. Gentry-controlled Parliament passes enclosure acts → dispossession → wage labor (primitive accumulation)
4. Navigation Acts + naval power → protects/expands merchant capital's markets
5. Bank of England + funded national debt (1694) → credit infrastructure financing 18th-c. wars and, eventually, industrial investment
- **Forward link:** this sequence — commodified land, secure property/credit, a dispossessed wage-labor force, protected merchant markets, and war/investment-grade public credit — is the precondition base later capital draws on to industrialize

*Caveat — contested reading*
- Revisionist historians (Russell, Kishlansky; Trevor-Roper vs. Tawney's "rise of the gentry" thesis) argue the revolution was driven by contingent religious/constitutional crisis (Charles I's mismanagement, the Scottish Bishops' Wars, court faction) rather than a coherent bourgeois class project — "class interest" may impose more retrospective coherence than the actors actually had

**American Revolution (1765–1783)**

*1. Ending British mercantile restriction on colonial trade and currency*
- Navigation Acts no longer bind the new states — merchants can trade directly with any nation, not just through England
  - Benefits colonial merchants previously boxed into England-only trade (many, like Hancock, had already evaded this via smuggling)
- Currency Act (1764) restriction lifted — states/Congress can issue paper money independent of London-controlled specie
  - Benefits merchants and debtors needing independent liquidity
- Sugar Act (1764) and Townshend Act (1767) tariffs no longer apply
  - Benefits New England merchants, rum distillers, importers

*2. Transferring Loyalist property to the Patriot elite*
- State-level Confiscation Acts (1770s–80s) seize and resell property of fled/exiled Loyalists
  - Benefits Patriot elites and speculators buying confiscated land cheap — a direct, one-time capital transfer

*3. Opening the western frontier through Native dispossession*
- Proclamation Line of 1763 becomes void — no more British cap on westward settlement
  - Benefits land speculators (Washington's own Ohio Company claims had been voided by this line)
- Northwest Ordinance (1787): organizes former Native land north of the Ohio River for settlement/statehood
  - Benefits settlers and land speculators; dispossesses Native nations

*4. Building a national market and a creditor-protective federal state*
- Shays' Rebellion (1786–87): debt-relief uprising by farmer-veterans, suppressed by merchant-funded militia → triggers the Constitutional Convention
- US Constitution (1787–89):
  - Contracts Clause (Art. I, Sec. 10): bars states from debtor-relief laws → benefits creditors/financiers, closes off the Shays'-style remedy
  - Commerce Clause (Art. I, Sec. 8): federal power over interstate/foreign trade → benefits merchants, creates one national market from thirteen
  - Full Faith and Credit Clause: debts enforceable across state lines → benefits interstate creditors/merchants
  - Federal taxing power + power to raise an army/suppress insurrection → benefits the propertied class generally

*5. National financial infrastructure*
- Hamilton's financial program (1790–91): federal assumption of state war debts, national Bank of the United States, funded public debt — modeled on the Bank of England (1694)
  - Benefits bondholders/speculators who bought depreciated war bonds cheap, now paid at face value; financiers generally

*6. Constitutional entrenchment of slavery*
- Three-Fifths Compromise (Art. I, Sec. 2): counts 3/5 of enslaved population for representation without rights → benefits Southern slaveholders, inflates their congressional power
- Slave Trade Clause (Art. I, Sec. 9): bars Congress from banning international slave trade until 1808 → benefits slave importers/plantation owners
- Fugitive Slave Clause (Art. IV, Sec. 2): requires return of escaped enslaved people across state lines → benefits slaveholders
- **Forward link:** liberal-constitutional order for property/commerce coexisting with a slave mode of production — contradiction only the Civil War forces open, eighty years later

*Agents*
- Merchants (Boston, NY, Philadelphia) — hit by Navigation/Sugar/Stamp/Tea Acts
- Planters (esp. Virginia) — Washington, Jefferson: land speculators, often indebted to British merchant-creditors
- Lawyers/professional class — Jefferson, Adams: supply ideological/legal case
- Artisans/urban crowds — Sons of Liberty, direct action (Tea Party) — radical element structurally similar in role to England's Levellers
- Loyalists — lose property via confiscation
- Enslaved people — many sided with Britain (Dunmore's Proclamation, 1775, offered freedom for joining British forces)
- Native nations — mostly allied with Britain, correctly anticipating faster dispossession under an independent US
- Debtor farmers (postwar) — Shays' Rebellion: patriots during the war, adversaries of the patriot elite once it's won

*Interests at play*
- Merchants: end mercantilist restriction, free trade, freedom from arbitrary taxation
- Planters: escape debt to British creditors, protect/expand slavery, access western land
- Creditors/financiers (postwar): a central government able to enforce contracts and suppress debtor unrest
- Debtor farmers: wanted paper money/debt relief — lost decisively in the constitutional settlement
- Enslaved people and Native nations: interests overridden by the settlement in both cases

**French Revolution (1789)**

*1. Abolishing feudal privilege — the August Decrees (Aug 4–11, 1789)*
- Abolishes seigneurial dues, the tithe (dîme), corvée labor, and venality of office
  - Benefits peasants (relief from feudal dues, though full abolition without buyout only comes in 1793) and the bourgeoisie (careers no longer blocked by inherited office)
  - Hurts nobility and clergy — their main income streams and legal privileges

*2. Legal equality and property as a natural right — Declaration of the Rights of Man (Aug 26, 1789)*
- Establishes legal equality, presumption of innocence, free speech/press; declares property "inviolable and sacred"
  - Benefits the propertied bourgeoisie primarily — legal equality and secured property are exactly capital's institutional needs
  - Excludes women (Olympe de Gouges's 1791 *Declaration of the Rights of Woman* protests this directly) and colonial subjects

*3. Nationalizing and privatizing Church land — Civil Constitution of the Clergy (1790) and sale of *biens nationaux**
- Confiscates Church property, subordinates clergy to the state, sells confiscated (and later émigré) land as *biens nationaux*
  - Benefits the state treasury (funds the revolution/war) and bourgeois/wealthier-peasant buyers, often bought up in large lots by urban speculators
  - Hurts the Church and poorer peasants largely priced out of the sales

*4. Freeing the market for capital while banning organized labor — Allarde Decree and Le Chapelier Law (1791)*
- Allarde Decree abolishes guilds and trade monopolies; Le Chapelier Law bans workers' associations, unions, and strikes in the same year
  - Benefits bourgeois manufacturers/employers — free market entry for capital, no organized counter-power from labor
  - Hurts artisans (lose guild protections) and wage workers (banned from collective bargaining)

*5. Building one national market*
- Eliminates internal tolls (*péages*) and provincial customs; unifies weights and measures (metric system, 1795)
  - Benefits merchants and manufacturers — a single national market instead of a provincial patchwork

*6. Suppressing the popular/sans-culotte wing — the Maximum and Thermidor*
- Law of the Maximum (Sept 1793): price controls, grain requisitioning, pushed by Jacobins under sans-culotte pressure
  - Benefits urban poor and soldiers dependent on requisitioned food; hurts merchants/farmers/speculators
- Thermidorian Reaction (July 1794): Robespierre executed, Jacobin dictatorship ends, Maximum abolished (Dec 1794)
  - Benefits the property-owning bourgeoisie — market liberalism restored
  - Hurts the urban poor — food-price inflation returns, feeding further unrest (Prairial uprising, 1795, crushed by the same authorities)
  - Same structural pattern as the Levellers/Diggers in England and Shays' Rebellion in America: a popular/radical wing pushes further than bourgeois leadership wants, and is suppressed *by* that leadership

*7. Formalizing property-based political power — Directory Constitution (Year III, 1795)*
- Introduces *censitaire* (property-qualified) suffrage — only tax-paying property owners can vote, only the wealthiest can hold office
  - Benefits the propertied bourgeoisie — political power now explicitly tied to wealth

*8. Codifying and exporting the new order — the Napoleonic Code (1804)*
- Codifies legal equality, secular civil law, contract and property rights across France and, via conquest, much of continental Europe
- Also entrenches patriarchal family law and, via a separate 1802 decree, restores colonial slavery, reversing the Convention's 1794 abolition
  - Benefits bourgeois property owners generally, men under family law, and colonial planters (slavery restored)
  - Hurts women (legally subordinated in marriage) and enslaved people — freedom won in 1794 is reversed in 1802, directly driving Haiti's fight for full independence (achieved 1804)

*Agents*
- Bourgeoisie (lawyers, merchants, financiers, officials) — Sieyès, Mirabeau, later Girondins (moderate, commercial) vs. Montagnards/Jacobins (radical, allied tactically with the sans-culottes)
- Peasantry — most numerous group; the Great Fear (summer 1789) directly forces the August Decrees
- Urban sans-culottes (artisans, shopkeepers, wage laborers) — drive the insurrectionary *journées* (Bastille, Women's March on Versailles, overthrow of the monarchy in 1792), push the revolution left of its bourgeois leadership
- Nobility and clergy — reactionary interest; many emigrate and organize counter-revolutionary armies
- Women — politically active (Women's March, Society of Revolutionary Republican Women) but denied citizenship; de Gouges executed 1793
- Enslaved people in the colonies — the Haitian Revolution as the radical extension of the metropole's own universalist language

*Interests at play*
- Bourgeoisie: legal equality, secure property, free market entry, political power proportional to wealth
- Peasantry: end of seigneurial dues/tithes, land ownership — only partially achieved, since poorer peasants mostly couldn't afford *biens nationaux*
- Urban sans-culottes: price controls, food security, broad political inclusion — largely defeated by Thermidor
- Nobility/clergy: defense of feudal privilege, restoration of monarchy
- Women and enslaved colonial subjects: legal/political equality and freedom — both promised by the Declaration's language, both denied or reversed in practice

*Adaptation chain to capitalism*
1. August Decrees (1789) → destroys feudal dues/privilege → land and labor freed from seigneurial obligation
2. Declaration of the Rights of Man → legal equality + property as sacred right → institutional foundation for capitalist contract/property law
3. Civil Constitution of the Clergy + sale of *biens nationaux* → transfers land to bourgeois/peasant buyers, expands the land market
4. Allarde Decree + Le Chapelier Law (1791) → opens market entry for capital, bans organized labor
5. Abolition of internal tolls/customs + unified measures → one national market
6. Thermidorian Reaction (1794) → abolishes price controls → restores market liberalism against popular economic constraint
7. Directory's *censitaire* suffrage (1795) → political power formally tied to property ownership
8. Napoleonic Code (1804) → codifies and exports this bourgeois legal order by conquest — while entrenching patriarchal family law and restoring colonial slavery

*Haitian Revolution (1791–1804)*
- Enslaved people take "liberty, equality" literally → defeat France/Britain/Spain → abolish slavery → found Haiti (James 1938)
  - Dialectical twist: revolution's own ideology exceeds bourgeois interests → Napoleon restores slavery elsewhere (1802)

**Net effect by 1800:** capitalist legal template (property, contract, formal equality) exists and spreads — feudal/slave relations persist elsewhere (continental Europe, US South) → unfinished business for the 19th century

## 2. Age of Revolution (1789–1848)
- Napoleon exports bourgeois law (Napoleonic Code) by conquest
  - Contradiction: authoritarian empire spreading liberal property relations
- Congress of Vienna (1814–15): political counter-revolution, doesn't fully reverse social transformation
  - → mismatch: conservative superstructure atop partly-modernized social relations
  - → engine of recurring revolutionary crises (Hobsbawm 1962)
- 1830 (France): Louis-Philippe's "bourgeois monarchy" replaces reactionary Charles X
  - Shift toward haute bourgeoisie — not yet democracy
- **1848 — decisive moment:**
  - Revolutions in France, German states, Austria, Italy, Hungary
  - Marx (*Communist Manifesto*, Feb 1848; *Class Struggles in France*; *18th Brumaire*): bourgeoisie needs worker support vs. aristocracy → recoils once workers raise independent demands
  - June Days (Paris): bourgeois-controlled National Guard crushes workers' uprising
  - By 1849: revolutions defeated politically
  - **Real significance:** proletariat emerges as an independent historical actor, opposed to the bourgeoisie → structures the rest of the century

---

## 3. Age of Capital (1848–1875)
- Bourgeoisie shifts: political revolution → economic hegemony
  - Railways, investment banking, free trade (1860 Cobden–Chevalier Treaty) (Hobsbawm 1975)
- Nation-building = market unification:
  - Germany: Zollverein customs union (1834) integrates markets decades before political unification (1871)
  - Bismarck: conservative elites co-opt nationalism from above → preempt independent liberal/worker revolution (≈ Gramsci's "passive revolution")
- US Civil War = "Second American Revolution":
  - Abolishes slave mode of production
  - Establishes single national market, free wage labor as dominant relation
  - Reconstruction overthrown: Compromise of 1877 → Northern capital trades Black political rights for Southern planter cooperation
  - Formal equality ≠ substantive equality (Du Bois 1935; Foner 1988)
- Pattern: nation-states built for uniform internal markets; nationalism = mobilizing ideology for the economic project
  - *Caveat:* liberal-nationalist historians stress nationalism's own independent force

---

## 4. Age of Empire (1875–1900, → 1914)
- Long Depression (1873–96) → squeezed profit rates
- Capital concentrates: cartels, trusts, bank–industry fusion = "finance capital" (Hilferding)
  - → Lenin (1917): imperialism = exporting surplus capital once domestic markets saturate
- Scramble for Africa / forced opening of Asia:
  - Driven by need for markets, raw materials, capital outlets — not just prestige
  - = new frontier for "primitive accumulation" (Marx 1867; Rodney 1972 on Africa)
- Uneven and combined development (Trotsky) explains Japan's Meiji trajectory:
  - Combines feudal residues + imported industrial/military tech → "revolution from above" → avoids colonization
- Proletariat organizes globally in response:
  - Second International (1889)
  - Mass socialist parties (German SPD), British labour movement, trade unionism
  - Capital's globalization → organizes its own opposition (Marx & Engels 1848)

---

## 5. Fin de Siècle → 1914
- Productive forces increasingly international: world market, transnational finance, global commodity chains (Beckert 2014, cotton)
- Political form still the rival nation-state
- Uneven development (Germany, US, Japan rising; Britain relatively declining) → inter-imperialist rivalry
  - Competition to redivide the colonial world
  - Arms race, rigid alliances (Triple Alliance vs. Triple Entente)
- Lenin (1917) / Bukharin: WWI = rival national capitals fighting to redivide the world market by force
- *Caveat:* miscalculation, alliance commitments, July Crisis 1914 decisions also mattered independently

---

## 6. Synthesis
- Long 19th century = capitalism becoming the dominant world mode of production
- Sequence:
  - Bourgeois revolutions (English, American, French) → establish capitalist property relations + state form
  - Age of Revolution / Age of Capital → consolidate industrial capitalism, complete national market unification (incl. uneven abolition of slavery)
  - Age of Empire → extend capitalism to its monopoly/finance-capital/imperialist stage, globally
- Each stage resolves an old contradiction, generates a new one:
  - Aristocracy vs. bourgeoisie → bourgeoisie vs. proletariat → rival national capitals
- Dialectical mechanism propels history → 20th-century crises: WWI, Russian Revolution (1917)

## 7. Marx's Intellectual Evolution (1818–1883)

**Formation — Young Hegelian, Journalist (1818–1843)**
- Born 1818, Trier, Prussia (Rhineland — more liberal, commercial than Prussian heartland)
- University of Berlin → joins Young Hegelians — uses Hegel's dialectic to critique religion/state, not defend it
- Editor, *Rheinische Zeitung* (1842–43)
  - Covers Rhineland debate on the Theft of Wood Law — peasants gathering fallen wood newly criminalized as landowners' property rights tighten
  - → first direct clash between abstract law and material class interest → pushes Marx toward political economy
  - Paper shut down by Prussian censorship, 1843

**Paris — Philosophical Break (1843–1845)**
- Moves to Paris → direct contact with French socialist/workers' movements
- *Introduction to Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right* (1844): identifies the proletariat as history's revolutionary class — "a class with radical chains"
- Meets Engels (1844) → lifelong collaboration begins
- Expelled from France (1845) → moves to Brussels

**Brussels — Historical Materialism Formalized (1845–1848)**
- *Theses on Feuerbach* (1845): breaks with passive materialism → "philosophers have only interpreted the world, the point is to change it"
- *The German Ideology* (1845–46, with Engels): formalizes historical materialism — social being determines consciousness, not the reverse
- Joins/reorganizes the Communist League (1847)
- *Communist Manifesto* (written 1847, published Feb 1848)
  - Commissioned as the League's program
  - Published the same month revolutions erupt across Europe
  - Core thesis: history = class struggle; bourgeoisie creates its own "gravediggers" (proletariat)

**Revolution and Defeat (1848–1850)**
- Edits *Neue Rheinische Zeitung* (1848–49) — supports the German revolution
- Expelled from Prussia, then France again → settles in London, 1849 (permanent exile)
- *The Class Struggles in France* (1850): explains 1848's failure — bourgeoisie fears the proletariat (June Days) → allies with reaction instead
- Communist League splits (1850): Marx/Engels (wait for ripe conditions) vs. Willich–Schapper (immediate insurrection) → League collapses, 1852

**London Exile — Political Economy (1850–1867)**
- *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte* (1852): analyzes Louis Napoleon's coup → concept of Bonapartism (state balancing rival class factions, gaining relative autonomy from all of them)
- *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy*, Preface (1859): clearest short statement of base/superstructure — mode of production shapes social, political, intellectual life
- *Capital*, Vol. 1 (1867): theory of surplus value, the commodity form, "so-called primitive accumulation" (enclosure, colonial plunder as capitalism's violent origin)
  - Only volume Marx finishes himself; Vols. 2–3 published posthumously by Engels

**International Organizing and Conflict (1864–1876)**
- First International founded, London, 1864 — Marx writes the Inaugural Address
- Internal conflict (coalition, not a Marxist party):
  - Proudhonists: resist strikes/political action, favor cooperative credit
  - Lassalleans: favor state-aided cooperatives, universal suffrage over revolution
  - Bakunin/anarchists: want immediate abolition of all state authority; Marx argues for a transitional workers' state
- Paris Commune, 1871: workers briefly govern Paris → crushed ("Bloody Week")
  - *The Civil War in France* (1871): Marx hails the Commune as the first glimpse of working-class self-government
- Hague Congress (1872): Bakunin expelled → International dissolved, 1876

**Final Years (1875–1883)**
- *Critique of the Gotha Programme* (1875, private; published 1891 by Engels)
  - Attacks Lassalle's "iron law of wages" and reliance on state-aided cooperatives
  - Distinguishes lower phase of communism (reward by labor) from higher phase ("to each according to his needs")
  - States a "revolutionary dictatorship of the proletariat" as the necessary transition
- Letter to Vera Zasulich (1881): allows Russia's peasant commune could skip full capitalist development, combined with Western revolution — qualifies any rigid "same stages for everyone" reading
- Marx dies March 14, 1883, London — *Capital* left unfinished
